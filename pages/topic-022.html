Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery

Transforming ambiguous stakeholder goals into measurable engineering specifications: functional vs non-functional '-ilities', SLAs, latency budgets, MoSCoW prioritization, and stakeholder mapping.

On this page
1 Real-World Explanation 2 Technical Discussion 3 Cloud City Analogy 4 Practice Exercise 5 Knowledge Check 6 Further Reading

1 Real-World Explanation

The Situation: Bridging the Business-Technical Chasm

At Brightloaf Bakery, the executive team announced plans to scale online ordering to 25 countries. The CEO told engineering: "Make it fast, reliable, and cheap." Without systematic requirements analysis, one engineer provisioned a multi-region active-active Spanner database costing $20,000/month, while another deployed a single micro-VM that crashed on day one. [1] [2]

The Lead Cloud Architect intervened by instituting formal Requirements Discovery. The team segregated Functional Requirements (what the system does: cart checkout, recipe search) from Non-Functional Requirements (how the system behaves: 99.95% monthly availability, p99 latency < 200ms, RTO < 15 min). [1] [2]

The Non-Functional '-ilities' Matrix
  • Availability: e.g., 99.95% uptime allows at most 21.9 minutes of unplanned downtime per month. [1] [2]
  • Scalability: Handling peak load spikes (e.g., 10x traffic increase during Black Friday morning) without manual intervention. [1] [2]
  • Durability: Cloud Storage 99.999999999% (11 9s) ensures data survives physical media failures. [1] [2]
  • Observability: Exporting SLI metrics, centralized audit logs, and distributed traces with sub-second ingestion. [1] [2]

Foundation references: Operational excellence pillar · Reliability pillar

The Pain

Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery becomes risky when teams treat Business requirements: revenue goals, time-to-market, growth plans, risk appetite, geography, brand and customer experience as a checkbox instead of a boundary with an owner, evidence, and recovery path. [1] [2]

The Idea in One Paragraph

Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery is a design decision anchored by Business requirements: revenue goals, time-to-market, growth plans, risk appetite, geography, brand and customer experience. Connect it to Technical requirements: throughput, latency, data volume, consistency, availability targets, integration points, make the dependency visible, and state what changes when the requirement or failure domain changes. [1] [2]

Real-World Examples

  • Brightloaf scenario: make Business requirements: revenue goals, time-to-market, growth plans, risk appetite, geography, brand and customer experience observable and reviewable before launch. [1] [2]
  • Brightloaf scenario: make Technical requirements: throughput, latency, data volume, consistency, availability targets, integration points observable and reviewable before launch. [1] [2]
  • Brightloaf scenario: make Functional vs non-functional requirements (the "-ilities": availability, scalability, durability, maintainability, security, observability) observable and reviewable before launch. [1] [2]

2 Technical Discussion & Architecture Diagrams

Layer 1 — Foundation: Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements define business workflows (user authentication, payment processing). Non-functional requirements (NFRs) dictate performance, security, compliance, and disaster recovery constraints. [1] [2]

Layer 2 — Practitioner: Quantifying Ambiguity & MoSCoW Prioritization

Never accept vague terms like "high availability". Convert them into: "System must sustain 5,000 requests/sec with p99 latency < 300ms across 2 GCP regions with zero data loss (RPO = 0)". Use MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) to protect release dates. [1] [2]

Layer 3 — Architect: Stakeholder Mapping & Conflict Resolution

Balancing conflicting stakeholder priorities: Finance wants lowest cost (OpEx reduction), Security demands zero external IPs and CMEK encryption, Product wants rapid feature delivery, and SRE demands strict error budgets. [1] [2]

Layer 4 — Staff: Strategic Enterprise Alignment & TCO

Staff architects validate long-term contracts, existing software licenses (Bring Your Own License for Windows/SQL Server), vendor lock-in trade-offs, and multi-year Total Cost of Ownership models. [1] [2]

Technical references: Operational excellence pillar · Reliability pillar

Scope and planes

The scope of Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery is set by Business requirements: revenue goals, time-to-market, growth plans, risk appetite, geography, brand and customer experience. Separate the control-plane decision from the runtime path, and state which organization, project, region, zone, or resource owns the boundary. [1] [2]

Practitioner defaults and troubleshooting

Start by verifying Technical requirements: throughput, latency, data volume, consistency, availability targets, integration points, then inspect the identity, quota, network, logging, and dependency evidence that can invalidate that assumption. [1] [2]

4 Practice Exercise

Practice Exercise: Discovery Interview to Technical Specification

A retail client states: "Our checkout system must never lose orders, even if a whole datacenter burns down. We expect 1,000 orders/sec during holiday sales. Customers complain if pages take more than 1 second to load."

Translate these business statements into: (1) Availability target, (2) RTO/RPO targets, (3) Latency SLI, and (4) GCP service selection candidates.

Architectural Specification Solution

  1. Availability Target: 99.99% monthly availability with multi-region failover.
  2. RTO / RPO: RTO < 5 minutes (automated failover); RPO = 0 (synchronous replication across regions).
  3. Latency SLI: p99 end-to-end checkout response time < 800ms at 1,000 RPS.
  4. GCP Candidates: Cloud Spanner multi-region instance for ACID order persistence (RPO=0), Cloud Run or GKE across multiple regions fronted by a Global External Application Load Balancer.
