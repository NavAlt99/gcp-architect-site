Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery

Transforming ambiguous stakeholder goals into measurable engineering specifications: functional vs non-functional '-ilities', SLAs, latency budgets, MoSCoW prioritization, and stakeholder mapping.

On this page
1 Real-World Explanation 2 Technical Discussion 3 Cloud City Analogy 4 Practice Exercise 5 Knowledge Check 6 Further Reading

1 Real-World Explanation

The Situation: Bridging the Business-Technical Chasm

At Brightloaf Bakery, the executive team announced plans to scale online ordering to 25 countries. The CEO told engineering: "Make it fast, reliable, and cheap." Without systematic requirements analysis, one engineer provisioned a multi-region active-active Spanner database costing $20,000/month, while another deployed a single micro-VM that crashed on day one.

The Lead Cloud Architect intervened by instituting formal Requirements Discovery. The team segregated Functional Requirements (what the system does: cart checkout, recipe search) from Non-Functional Requirements (how the system behaves: 99.95% monthly availability, p99 latency < 200ms, RTO < 15 min).

The Non-Functional '-ilities' Matrix
  • Availability: e.g., 99.95% uptime allows at most 21.9 minutes of unplanned downtime per month.
  • Scalability: Handling peak load spikes (e.g., 10x traffic increase during Black Friday morning) without manual intervention.
  • Durability: Cloud Storage 99.999999999% (11 9s) ensures data survives physical media failures.
  • Observability: Exporting SLI metrics, centralized audit logs, and distributed traces with sub-second ingestion.

The Pain

Without a deliberate Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery design, Brightloaf can see slow pages, failed orders, surprise spend, audit gaps, or a deployment that requires a weekend of manual work.

The Idea in One Paragraph

Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery gives one business problem a clear boundary, an observable lifecycle, and an explicit recovery choice. The learner should be able to explain the idea before seeing configuration.

Real-World Examples

  • Retail: a seasonal order spike must not become a checkout outage.
  • Healthcare: a records workflow must preserve privacy and an audit trail.
  • Media or gaming: a bursty workload must scale without making every dependency global.

2 Technical Discussion & Architecture Diagrams

Layer 1 — Foundation: Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements define business workflows (user authentication, payment processing). Non-functional requirements (NFRs) dictate performance, security, compliance, and disaster recovery constraints.

Layer 2 — Practitioner: Quantifying Ambiguity & MoSCoW Prioritization

Never accept vague terms like "high availability". Convert them into: "System must sustain 5,000 requests/sec with p99 latency < 300ms across 2 GCP regions with zero data loss (RPO = 0)". Use MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) to protect release dates.

Layer 3 — Architect: Stakeholder Mapping & Conflict Resolution

Balancing conflicting stakeholder priorities: Finance wants lowest cost (OpEx reduction), Security demands zero external IPs and CMEK encryption, Product wants rapid feature delivery, and SRE demands strict error budgets.

Layer 4 — Staff: Strategic Enterprise Alignment & TCO

Staff architects validate long-term contracts, existing software licenses (Bring Your Own License for Windows/SQL Server), vendor lock-in trade-offs, and multi-year Total Cost of Ownership models.

Scope and planes

The resource model for Requirements Analysis: Business & Technical Discovery must name its organization, folder, project, global, regional, zonal, or resource scope. Control-plane intent and data-plane traffic are different paths.

Practitioner defaults and troubleshooting

Set explicit project, region, identity, quotas, logging, and network dependencies. Treat undocumented internals as observed behaviour, may change.

Design-review questions

  1. What business requirement selects this service?
  2. What is its real scope?
  3. Which control-plane calls must succeed?
  4. What is the data-plane failure signal?
  5. Which quota or IP range saturates first?
  6. What is the blast radius of a bad policy?
  7. How are cost and usage attributed?
  8. How is rollback or migration tested?

  • Explicit scope and ownership
  • Least-privilege identity
  • Observable health and cost
  • Tested recovery and rollback
  • Documented alternative and exit path

Architecture diagrams in this curriculum are not decorative illustrations; they are precision engineering models designed to answer three distinct, non-overlapping architectural questions for every topic:

D1 — Scope & Boundary Map (Structural Lens)

Question Answered: Where do execution and security boundaries lie?
Maps components into their isolation perimeters (Client Edge vs Project VPC vs Managed Services). Establishes trust boundaries, network ingress choke-points, and data-plane vs control-plane separation.

D2 — Request & Control Flow (Runtime Ingress Lens)

Question Answered: How does production traffic traverse the architecture securely?
Traces an end-to-end operational journey—from ingress initiation and IAM perimeter validation across Andromeda SDN to backend state persistence and audit logging.

D3 — Failure Injection & Resilience (Chaos Lens)

Question Answered: How does the system degrade, alert, and auto-heal under catastrophic failure?
Models the exact mechanics of component saturation, network timeouts, and health check evictions under stress, revealing observability signals and automated failover pathways.

Practice Exercise: Discovery Interview to Technical Specification

A retail client states: "Our checkout system must never lose orders, even if a whole datacenter burns down. We expect 1,000 orders/sec during holiday sales. Customers complain if pages take more than 1 second to load."

Translate these business statements into: (1) Availability target, (2) RTO/RPO targets, (3) Latency SLI, and (4) GCP service selection candidates.

Architectural Specification Solution

  1. Availability Target: 99.99% monthly availability with multi-region failover.
  2. RTO / RPO: RTO < 5 minutes (automated failover); RPO = 0 (synchronous replication across regions).
  3. Latency SLI: p99 end-to-end checkout response time < 800ms at 1,000 RPS.
  4. GCP Candidates: Cloud Spanner multi-region instance for ACID order persistence (RPO=0), Cloud Run or GKE across multiple regions fronted by a Global External Application Load Balancer.
LIVE DEMO SAFETY: T2 by default. Set DEMO_PROJECT_ID, confirm the active project, use a budget alert, and do not run apply until the plan is reviewed.
